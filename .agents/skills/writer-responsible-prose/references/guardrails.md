# Guardrails

Read this before rewriting anyone's text. The other files say what to kill; this one says what to keep and when a tell is not a tell.

## The rewrite contract

- Keep every claim. Shorten dull parts, expand useful parts, merge or split paragraphs, and keep the information through every structural change.
- Never invent a fact, name, number, date, quote, or citation. If a sentence needs a missing detail, ask for it or write a simpler sentence. Fiction is exempt, since invented detail is the task there.
- You may add an opinion or a reaction when the writer's voice calls for one. You may not add a factual claim.
- After rewriting, ask two questions: what still sounds generated, and did the rewrite add or drop any claim. Treat either failure as an error, then fix it.

## Match the writer

When the user provides a sample of their own writing, read it before touching the draft. Note sentence length, word choice, paragraph openings, punctuation, repeated phrases, and transitions, then match those habits. The sample overrides every rule in this skill where they conflict, including the dash rule: if the sample uses em dashes, keep them at the sample's rate.

Add personality only where it fits. Blog posts, essays, opinion, and personal writing take the writer's humor, uncertainty, and asides. Reference, technical, legal, and factual text stays neutral, so no first-person color where it does not belong.

## What not to flag

None of these proves generation by itself:

- Perfect grammar and consistent style. Many writers are professionals, or were edited.
- Bland prose without specific tells. Dry writing is just dry.
- Formal or academic words outside the watch lists. Simplify the listed words, never every big word.
- A single transition word. *However* or *additionally* alone is ordinary; the tell is the pileup.
- Curly quotes alone, since most editors auto-curl.
- Em dashes alone. Plenty of journalists love them; dashes count as evidence only next to formulaic rhythm.
- One short sentence for emphasis. Flag fragments only in rows.
- Deliberate repeated openings that build pressure, as in `She came. She saw. She conquered.` Change repetition only when it adds nothing.
- Scope statements, safety notices, real corrections, named objections, replies, and FAQ answers. These are useful limits, so they stay.
- Real alternatives a reader would actually weigh in a design doc, tutorial, or argument.
- Unsourced claims. Most of the web is unsourced, and missing citations prove nothing.
- Clean, complex formatting. Templates and visual editors produce it without any model involved.
- Text written before December 2022, which predates the pattern.

When unsure, look for clusters. One tell proves nothing, and several stock patterns in one passage are strong evidence.

## Secondhand text

Never rewrite watched phrases inside quotations, titles, proper names, or examples where the phrase is discussed rather than used. The `Bad:` examples throughout this skill exist under exactly that carve-out.

## Human details to keep

These carry the writer's voice, so keep them unless they damage the meaning:

- Specific, unusual details (`the lawyer who used to work upstairs from my dentist`).
- Mixed feelings and unresolved tension (`this is mostly good, but it bothers me, and I can't fully explain why`).
- Era-bound slang, memes, and in-jokes, which models lag on by a year or more.
- Deliberate first-person choices the writer can defend.
- Uneven sentence length. Real prose alternates short and long.
- Genuine asides, parentheticals, and self-corrections, which models rarely produce.
